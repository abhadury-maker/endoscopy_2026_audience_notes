Session Companion Notes

What MedTech Can Learn from Pharma

Brad Maruca, Managing Director, Deloitte
Annual Advances & Innovations in Endoscopic Oncology · October 2026
Educational Objectives

After this session, attendees will be able to:

1

Compare

The FDA 510(k) device clearance pathway to the pharmaceutical NDA process and describe the implications for the clinical evidence base underlying endoscopic technologies

2

Explain

How the absence of pre-launch health economics and reimbursement infrastructure creates downstream barriers to procedure adoption, training, and patient access

3

Identify

The structural factors — reimbursement, volume, and training pipeline — that contribute to the ESD adoption gap between the United States and Asia-Pacific

4

Describe

How real-world evidence generated through GI registries can be used to support payer coverage arguments and CPT/APC policy engagement

5

Apply

A framework for evaluating new endoscopic technologies that accounts for the evidence standard, reimbursement pathway, and post-market data commitment

Pharma and MedTech are both trying to put better tools in clinicians' hands. They operate with fundamentally different systems for evidence, access, and adoption — and that gap has real consequences.

In endoscopic oncology, where the choice is between organ-sparing resection and surgery, this is not a systems inefficiency. It is a cancer care access problem.

The question is not whether endoscopy should become pharma. It is what pharma learned that endoscopy still needs to build — and what endoscopic oncology must build its own way.

Section 1

The Evidence Gap

A drug reaching your formulary required Phase I–III randomized controlled trials, head-to-head against standard of care. An endoscopic device can reach your unit through the FDA 510(k) pathway — which requires only substantial equivalence to a predicate device, with no head-to-head clinical trial required.

The question to ask
When a new endoscopic device arrives in your unit, ask: what is the post-market evidence, and how does this compare to the current standard of care? The 510(k) clearance letter does not answer that question.
The nuance
The 510(k) pathway's speed has real value — device innovation that required Phase III trials before clearance would have delayed tools that genuinely work. The goal is not to make medtech become pharma. It is to apply the same rigor through evidence pathways designed for devices.
Section 2

The Reimbursement Maze

Pharma builds its payer case before launch — health economics models, cost-effectiveness data, J-codes and NDCs that enable billing from day one. Most MedTech companies build the clinical story first and figure out reimbursement years later.

The POEM example
POEM was introduced in the U.S. around 2010–2012. A standalone CPT code (43497) was not established until January 2022 — nearly a decade of billing uncertainty. During that time, patient access depended entirely on whether the institution was willing to absorb that uncertainty.

The doom loop: Weak clinical data at launch sets off a chain reaction that locks in every downstream problem.

1

No ironclad clinical data

510(k) cleared without head-to-head RCT vs. standard of care

2

Weak payer case

No HEOR pre-launch; payers default to bundling or denial

3

Inadequate reimbursement

No physician CPT for ESD; unlisted codes; 8.3% denial rate

4

Volume drought

U.S.: fewer than 200 trained ESD physicians; fewer than 50 active centers

5

Never reach skills plateau

250–350 cases to competency untutored in U.S. vs. 30–40 supervised in Japan

6

Poor outcomes & long procedure times

Early-career complications confirm payer skepticism — loop repeats

The ESD economics
An endoscopist choosing between a 2-hour ESD and four colonoscopies faces a rational economic choice — four colonoscopies generate more RVU income in the same time, with reliable billing. There is no dedicated physician CPT code for ESD. The payment system has encoded a preference for the simpler procedure. Japan and Korea avoid this because their salaried hospital model removes the personal financial penalty for spending two hours on a complex resection.
Section 3

Adoption & Diffusion

Japan reached over 85% ESD penetration. The U.S. remains in single digits. The evidence is the same. The infrastructure is not.

The learning curve gap
In the U.S., an untutored endoscopist requires approximately 250–350 ESD procedures to reach competency benchmarks. In Japan and Korea, with structured mentorship, trainees achieve competency in 30–40 supervised cases. Same procedure. The difference is volume infrastructure and expert mentorship — itself a downstream consequence of the reimbursement environment.
Section 4

The Path Forward

GI endoscopy generates approximately 100 million colonoscopies per year in the United States. AI-assisted endoscopy (CADe/CADx) is creating procedure-level structured data at scale — every AI-assisted scope becomes a data point. This is the evidence infrastructure pharma took 30 years to build. Endoscopy can build it faster because the volume already exists.

1

Participate in registries

GIQuIC and GQUIC enrollment gives your outcomes institutional and national weight. The data you generate today becomes the payer argument in 2027. This is the highest-leverage action available to the clinicians in this room.

2

Engage policy and coverage

ASGE committee participation shapes CPT/APC arguments. This is how codes get written. When ASGE asks for committee volunteers, say yes.

3

Collaborate with industry early

Manufacturers who engage GI societies, registries, and academic endoscopists at study design — not after clearance — build better studies and better products. Ask for the post-market evidence commitment, not just the 510(k) summary.

Key Takeaways

What Closing the Gap Requires

1 · The evidence bar is not equal

Most endoscopic devices cleared via 510(k) have no head-to-head trial data. Scrutinize the evidence behind new tools — not just their FDA status. Push for rigorous evidence in the right form for the technology, not a weaker version of the pharma standard.

2 · Reimbursement is a clinical strategy issue

Coverage gaps determine which patients get access. The doom loop starts with weak evidence and ends with poor outcomes. Understanding the payer pathway — CPT codes, APC assignments, coverage with evidence development — is a clinical advocacy tool, not a billing department concern.

3 · The skills gap is structural, not individual

250 cases to competency without mentorship versus 30 with it. The difference is not physician talent — it is volume infrastructure. That infrastructure is built through registries, payment reform, and training investment.

4 · Your data is the lever

At ~100M colonoscopies per year, GI generates enough real-world volume to rival clinical trials. Registry participation is the highest-leverage action available. The question is not whether medtech should become pharma — it is what pharma learned that we still need to build, and what endoscopic oncology must build differently.
